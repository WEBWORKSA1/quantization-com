What to listen for

  • 100% strength, 1/16 grid: perfectly on the grid. Tight, but with every hit at a slightly different velocity it can still sound alive. Compare it with the loose take — this is the classic "machine" feel.
  • 50–70% strength: each note moves part of the way to the grid. The worst timing errors disappear while some of the player's push and pull survives. Many producers stop here.
  • Swing 58–62%: every second 16th note is delayed slightly. The hi-hats start to bounce without sounding like a shuffle.
  • Swing 66%: close to a triplet shuffle. At 100 BPM a 16th note lasts 150 ms; 66% swing delays the off-beat 16ths by about 48 ms.
  • Wrong grid: quantize a straight beat to 1/8 triplets and hear notes snap to the wrong places. Always match the grid to the smallest subdivision the part really uses.

The swing convention here follows classic drum machines: the percentage is the share of each pair of grid steps taken by the first note, so 50% means straight. Some DAWs label the same amounts on a 0–100% scale instead. Read the full music quantize guide for a conversion table.

Frequently asked questions

What does quantize strength do?

It sets how far each note moves toward the nearest grid line, as a percentage of the distance. At 100% the note lands exactly on the grid; at 50% it moves halfway; at 0% nothing changes.

How do I calculate the length of a 16th note?

Divide 60,000 by the tempo to get one beat in milliseconds, then divide by four. At 120 BPM a beat is 500 ms and a 16th note is 125 ms.

Should I quantize everything?

Usually not. Quantize the parts that define the pulse — kick, snare, bass — more firmly, and leave hi-hats, percussion and melodic parts looser so the groove keeps some human movement.
