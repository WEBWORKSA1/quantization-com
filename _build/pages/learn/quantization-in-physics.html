In physics, quantization means a quantity can take only certain discrete values. For bound systems such as an electron in an atom, energy comes in fixed levels, not a continuous range. It started with Planck's E = hf in 1900 and now underlies atomic spectra, lasers and semiconductors. This guide covers the key results, works two calculations, and separates physical from digital quantization.

Planck and Einstein: energy in packets of hf

Blackbody radiation and E = nhf

Classical physics could not explain the light spectrum of a hot object, a blackbody. The Rayleigh–Jeans law predicted intensity rising without limit at short wavelengths, the "ultraviolet catastrophe", while experiments showed the spectrum peaking and falling. In 1900 Max Planck matched the data by assuming that oscillators in the cavity walls hold energy only in whole multiples of a unit proportional to their frequency:

E = n h f    (n = 1, 2, 3, …)

f is the oscillator frequency and h is Planck's constant. Since the 2019 revision of the SI, h is defined as exactly 6.626 070 15 × 10−34 J·s, and the kilogram is defined from it.

The step hf is tiny at everyday scales. A green photon at 550 nm has f = c/λ ≈ 5.45 × 1014 Hz and energy hf ≈ 3.61 × 10−19 J, or about 2.25 eV. A 1 mW green laser pointer emits about 2.8 × 1015 photons per second, which is why light looks continuous. Shortcut: with hc ≈ 1,239.84 eV·nm, photon energy in eV ≈ 1,239.84 / λ in nm.

The photoelectric effect: light is quantized too

Planck quantized the oscillators. In 1905 Einstein went further and proposed that light itself arrives in quanta, now called photons, each carrying energy hf. That explained the photoelectric effect, where light ejects electrons from a metal surface:

Kmax = h f − φ

Kmax is the maximum kinetic energy of an ejected electron; φ is the work function, the minimum energy needed to free an electron from that metal.

None of the key observations fits a classical wave picture. Below a cutoff frequency fc = φ/h no electrons leave, however bright the light. Brighter light gives more electrons, not faster ones. Emission starts with no measurable delay even in dim light, because one photon delivers all its energy to one electron.

Worked example: sodium under violet light

Sodium's work function is about 2.46 eV, so its cutoff wavelength is 1,239.84 / 2.46 ≈ 504 nm. Violet light at 400 nm carries 1,239.84 / 400 ≈ 3.10 eV per photon, so the fastest electrons leave with Kmax ≈ 3.10 − 2.46 = 0.64 eV. Red light at 650 nm (1.91 eV) ejects nothing at any intensity.

Hydrogen energy levels and the Bohr model

Hydrogen gas does not glow with a continuous rainbow; it emits sharp lines at specific wavelengths. In 1885 Johann Balmer found an empirical formula for the visible lines. In 1913 Niels Bohr explained them by quantizing the electron's orbital angular momentum to L = nħ, where ħ = h/2π. Combined with Coulomb attraction, that condition gives a ladder of allowed energies:

En = −13.6 eV / n2    (n = 1, 2, 3, …)

The constant is the Rydberg energy, 13.605 693 eV in CODATA 2022, computed for an infinitely heavy nucleus. Accounting for the proton's finite mass (the reduced-mass correction) gives 13.598 eV, the measured ionization energy of hydrogen. The negative sign means the electron is bound; E = 0 is the threshold for escaping the atom.

The levels crowd together as n grows: E1 = −13.61 eV, E2 = −3.40 eV, E3 = −1.51 eV, E4 = −0.85 eV. Above zero the electron is free and its energy is continuous: quantization comes from confinement.

Transitions and the Rydberg formula

When an electron drops from level ni to a lower level nf, it emits a photon carrying the energy difference. Dividing by hc gives the Rydberg formula:

1/λ = R (1/nf2 − 1/ni2)

R∞ = 10,973,731.568 m−1 (CODATA 2022). Transitions ending at nf = 1 form the ultraviolet Lyman series; nf = 2 is the Balmer series, whose first lines are visible; nf = 3 is the infrared Paschen series.

Worked example: the n = 3 → 2 photon (H-alpha)

  • ΔE = 13.6057 eV × (1/22 − 1/32) = 13.6057 × (1/4 − 1/9) = 13.6057 × 5/36 = 1.890 eV
  • λ = hc / ΔE = 1,239.84 eV·nm / 1.8897 eV ≈ 656.1 nm
  • Same result from the Rydberg formula: 1/λ = 10,973,731.6 × 5/36 = 1,524,129 m−1, so λ ≈ 656.1 nm.

This is the red Balmer-alpha (H-alpha) line. The reduced-mass correction moves it to about 656.47 nm, close to the measured 656.46 nm in vacuum (656.28 nm in air). The shortest Balmer line (ni → ∞) is at about 364.6 nm.

The Bohr model gets hydrogen's energy levels right, but its picture of planet-like orbits is wrong. In full quantum mechanics the ground state has zero orbital angular momentum, not ħ, and the electron has no definite path. Use Bohr for the energy ladder and the quantum numbers below for everything else.

Particle in a box: quantization from confinement

The simplest fully quantum example is a particle trapped between two impenetrable walls a distance L apart. The wavefunction must vanish at both walls, so only standing waves with a whole number of half-wavelengths fit, like the modes of a guitar string:

En = n2 h2 / (8 m L2) = n2 π2 ħ2 / (2 m L2)    (n = 1, 2, 3, …)

Two lessons follow. The lowest energy is not zero; n = 0 would mean no wave at all, so a confined particle always keeps some zero-point motion. And the spacing scales as 1/(mL2): quantization is dramatic for light particles in small spaces and negligible for anything large.

Worked example: an electron in a 1 nm box

  • h2 = (6.626 070 15 × 10−34)2 = 4.3905 × 10−67 J2·s2
  • 8mL2 = 8 × 9.109 384 × 10−31 kg × (1 × 10−9 m)2 = 7.2875 × 10−48 kg·m2
  • E1 = 4.3905 × 10−67 / 7.2875 × 10−48 = 6.025 × 10−20 J
  • In electronvolts: 6.025 × 10−20 / 1.602 177 × 10−19 ≈ 0.376 eV
  • E2 = 4E1 ≈ 1.504 eV and E3 = 9E1 ≈ 3.384 eV

A 2 → 1 transition releases 3E1 ≈ 1.128 eV, a near-infrared photon at about 1,099 nm. Thermal energy at room temperature is kBT ≈ 0.026 eV, so these levels are well resolved. In a 1 cm box E1 falls by 1014, to about 4 × 10−15 eV. Quantum dots exploit this scaling: changing a nanocrystal's size changes its colour.

Quantized angular momentum and quantum numbers

Solving the Schrödinger equation for hydrogen in three dimensions gives the same energies as Bohr's model. It also shows that several quantities are quantized together, each labelled by a quantum number:

Quantum numberAllowed valuesWhat it fixes
Principal, n1, 2, 3, …Energy level (in hydrogen, energy depends on n alone)
Orbital, l0, 1, …, n − 1Orbital angular momentum magnitude, L = √(l(l+1)) ħ
Magnetic, ml−l, …, 0, …, +lComponent along a chosen axis, Lz = ml ħ
Spin, ms+½ or −½Electron spin component, Sz = ms ħ

That gives n2 orbital states per level, doubled by spin to 2n2: 2, 8, 18, 32. With the Pauli exclusion principle (one electron per complete set of quantum numbers), this counting underlies the periodic table. Angular momentum is quantized twice over: its magnitude takes only the values √(l(l+1))ħ, and its projection on any axis only whole multiples of ħ.

The quantum harmonic oscillator

A mass on an ideal spring, or an atom vibrating in a molecule, is a harmonic oscillator. Quantum mechanics gives it evenly spaced levels:

En = (n + ½) ħω    (n = 0, 1, 2, …)

ω = 2πf is the angular frequency. Adjacent levels differ by exactly ħω = hf; the ground state keeps a zero-point energy of ħω/2.

This is Planck's picture made exact: the oscillator exchanges energy only in units of hf, plus a zero-point term he did not include. Equal steps mean a vibrating molecule absorbs one characteristic infrared frequency per mode; real molecules deviate at higher n. Each mode of the electromagnetic field is also an oscillator, which leads to the last section.

Physical, digital and "second" quantization

The word quantization is used in three different senses that are easy to confuse.

  • Physical quantization is a property of nature. A bound system has only certain allowed values of energy or angular momentum. Measuring the system finds one of those values, and nothing has been rounded or lost.
  • Digital quantization is an engineering choice. A converter or a compressed AI model rounds a continuous value to the nearest of a finite set of levels and accepts a known error, ±½ step, as the price. The levels are chosen by people, not by nature. See ADC quantization error and what is quantization.
  • Canonical and second quantization are procedures for building quantum theories. Canonical quantization turns classical position and momentum into operators that obey [x̂, p̂] = iħ. Second quantization applies the same idea to fields, describing states by how many particles occupy each mode, with creation and annihilation operators. The name is a historical misnomer: nothing is quantized twice.

The common thread is discreteness; the cause differs. Physical levels come from boundary conditions on waves, digital levels from finite storage.

Key takeaways

  • Planck's E = nhf (1900) introduced energy quanta; h = 6.626 070 15 × 10−34 J·s exactly since 2019.
  • The photoelectric effect, Kmax = hf − φ, shows that light itself comes in photons of energy hf.
  • Hydrogen levels follow En = −13.6057 eV / n2; the 3 → 2 transition emits H-alpha at about 656 nm.
  • A particle in a box has En = n2h2/(8mL2): an electron in a 1 nm box has E1 ≈ 0.376 eV.
  • Quantum numbers n, l, ml and ms label the allowed states, 2n2 per hydrogen level.
  • The harmonic oscillator has equal steps of ħω and a zero-point energy of ħω/2.
  • Physical quantization is a property of nature; digital quantization is a rounding choice made by engineers.

Frequently asked questions

Is all energy quantized?

No. Only bound or confined systems have discrete levels. A free electron, or one with enough energy to leave an atom, can have any kinetic energy. That is why hydrogen's spectrum has sharp lines plus a continuum beyond the ionization limit.

Why is the hydrogen ground state −13.6 eV and not −13.6057 eV?

Both are roundings. The Rydberg energy for an infinitely heavy nucleus is 13.6057 eV; including the proton's finite mass gives 13.598 eV, which matches the measured ionization energy. Textbooks round either to 13.6 eV.

Why can't a particle in a box have zero energy?

Zero energy would require n = 0, which makes the wavefunction zero everywhere, meaning no particle. Equivalently, confining a particle to a region of size L gives it a momentum spread of order h/L, so it always has some kinetic energy.

Why is h now exact?

Since 20 May 2019 the SI defines the kilogram by fixing h at 6.626 070 15 × 10−34 J·s. It is a defined value, so it has no measurement uncertainty.
